The town

Labateca has existed,
in writing, since 1623

On 19 July of that year, in a place the Spanish had named the Valley of the Madmen, the scattered indigenous peoples of the valley were gathered into a single settlement. That act —signed, sealed and kept in Colombia's National Archive— is the legal birth of this municipality.

In short

The history in three minutes

Before anyone arrived from outside, these mountains were already inhabited. The Chitareros held the high ground, following the courses of rivers and streams; the Laches, or U'wa-Tunebo, held the lower, warmer land to the east. By the chronicles' account they were peaceable people —though not always with one another: those of Chucarima waged a war to the death with the Semayetas over farmland. From their language we still keep the names we use today: Labateca, Bochagá, Chitagá, Cúcuta.

In February 1550 a company of sixty or seventy men entered the valley under captain Pedro de Ursúa, settlers from the newly founded city of Pamplona. They had not come to settle: they had come to survey the territory and parcel out in encomienda whatever people they found. They were disappointed — there was no gold here. The valley's name was born of that encounter: the Spanish saw the local people make "fierce show and great feeling, with grimaces and loud cries", and christened it the Valley of the Madmen.

What followed was harsh, and it is documented without varnish: encomiendas, forced labour, sugar cane and wheat for the encomenderos, and a collapse in population. In 1559, when the notary Cristóbal Bueno came through to count, there remained 4,404 souls of what had probably been ten thousand.

There was a first attempt before the one everyone remembers. In April 1602 the inspector Antonio Beltrán de Guevara ordered the groups of Mónega, Chona and Móluga brought together on a plain called Locavita, with a doctrine chapel forty feet long. It did not last: twenty years later those chapels were ordered pulled down.

On 19 July 1623 the judge Juan de Villabona y Zubiaurre arrived. His decision was to gather all the scattered peoples of the valley onto a single site and raise a single doctrine, "complete, large and stable". The settling magistrate Luis Jurado rose "between two lights" to lay out with cords the square plaza and the straight streets six varas wide, and required the indigenous people to build thirty-two thatched houses for their chiefs, arranged in quarters by town of origin so that kinship would not be broken. So was born the Pueblo de Indios and Doctrine of Labateca, with its reserve lands and —this is always forgotten— with its own indigenous council, which elected its own mayors and aldermen.

In 1633, thirteen chiefs founded the Brotherhood of Our Lady of Sorrows. It was not only devotion: the brotherhood came to own a cattle herd and a cacao grove, and to have more than five thousand pesos lent out at interest. When in 1778 the inspector Moreno y Escandón ordered the reserve dissolved and the land auctioned off, it was those accounts that let the indigenous community appeal to the Royal Audiencia — and win. Labateca was one of the last Andean reserves to survive intact.

After that came the separation of Toledo in 1796, coffee, the passage from parish district to municipality, and the first popular election of a mayor in 1986.

Four centuries later, the town is still here. With other trades and other surnames, but in the same valley.

Why some say twelve, others thirteen, and this page says fifteen

All three figures have been written in Labateca, and it is not that anyone got it wrong. The confusion comes from the fact that a town and an encomienda are not the same thing, and the old lists mix the two.

  • A town was a place with its people: a hill, a river, some fields, a chief. You could point at it.
  • An encomienda was a piece of paper: a Spaniard's right to collect tribute and labour from a group of indigenous people. It was not the land, it was the people.

And the paperwork kept splitting. When an encomendero died, his encomienda was divided among his children; when a daughter married, a portion went as dowry. Within two generations the people of a single town could be divided among three different Spaniards without anyone having moved house.

Bochagá is the clear case. On the ground it was one town, on the bank of one river. In the act of 1623 it appears three times, because its people were granted to three lords: Alonso Gago de la Cerbela, Juan Durán and Alonso Carrillo. The same happened to Ima, which appears another three times. That is six of the fifteen lines in the list above — and they are two towns, not six.

So, depending on what you count, different numbers come out. All three are correct; what changes is the question:

  • 12how many places were there? What indigenous memory holds, going by the land and not by the bureaucracy: it merged the repeated names back together.
  • 15how many titles did the Crown parcel out? The encomienda seats gathered by the judge's act of 19 July 1623. That is the list above, and it is the list of this act.
  • 21how many entries did the land register have? The allotments recorded by the colonial administration, with the subdivisions already settled.

Names survive from those divisions that are still heard today. "Gago" was not a town: it was what people called the portion of Bochagá granted to Alonso Gago de la Cerbela. "Leutas" and "Tecasquimas" went on naming the groups of the high veredas that today are Balsa and Santa María.

The book tells it in Milestone I of chapter II, and the original division is in the 1623 act, page by page — both in Spanish.

The patron saint

The Virgin who saved the reserve

A canvas that appeared, a brotherhood of chiefs and a lawsuit won against the Crown. The story of the image that still presides over the church on the square.

Two stories are told about how the Virgin came to Labateca. The one the town repeats says she appeared to an indigenous woman surnamed Berbesí who was washing in a river. The one that was written down was told by Tomás Bochagá, an indigenous man: some of the old people found a canvas in the town of Bochagá and, it being "a thing never seen", they kept it; later a married couple, Francisco and María, handed it to the doctrine priest. That priest was friar Francisco de Alba — the same man who signs as parish priest of the doctrine in the act of 1623.

On 4 July 1633 the chiefs of the town founded the brotherhood of Our Lady of Sorrows. On paper it was a religious confraternity; in practice it was the one institution where indigenous people elected their own officers, handled common money and decided the town's affairs. The posts of steward and sacristan were reserved for women, and the sacristan was indigenous.

The feast lasted three days and the procession was shared out: the first day for the Spanish, the second for the town's own people and the third for outsiders. With the money from its cattle, the brotherhood paid in full for the repair of the parish church throughout the eighteenth century.

And in 1778 came the trial by fire. The Crown ordered the town erased: the people of Labateca were to move to Cácota de Velasco and the reserve would be sold to whites and mestizos. The indigenous community carried the Virgin in procession for several days and at the same time fought the case before the King's courts, proving with the brotherhood's accounts that the town had the means to support itself.

They won. Labateca kept its lands and remained one of the last indigenous reserves in Santander. The image is still in the church on the square today — the same one first seen on father Alba's cane frame.
