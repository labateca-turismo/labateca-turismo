The Virgin Who Saved the Land

Our Lady of Sorrows and the confraternity of the Indian town of Labateca

Based on the research of Carmen Adriana Ferreira Esparza, Universidad Industrial de Santander

  • Confraternity founded in 1633
  • Lawsuit won in 1778
  • Parish Archive of Labateca, Book of Confraternities 001

A canvas no one had ever seen

There are two stories about how the Virgin came to Labateca, and both are still told.

The first is the one the indigenous people themselves told, and the one still repeated in town today: the Virgin appeared to an indigenous woman named Berbesí who was washing clothes in a river. It belongs to the same family of stories as Our Lady of Guadalupe in Mexico or Our Lady of Charity in Cuba — an apparition to a humble person, beside water, in the years when the new religion was only beginning to take hold.

The second was written down. An indigenous man named Tomás Bochagá declared that, for as long as he could remember, he had heard his elders say that in one of the towns of the district — Bochagá — "the ancients" had found a painted canvas. Because it was to them a thing never before seen, they kept it. Later two married indigenous people, Francisco and María, gave it as a gift to the mission priest.

That priest was Friar Francisco de Alba. The same name that signs as priest of the new doctrine in the act by which Labateca was founded in 1623. The two documents — the lawsuit and the founding — meet in that one person.

According to the account, the priest had a frame made of cane, stretched the canvas over it and set it up high. Before long everyone was coming to see the Virgin, lighting her "with poor lamps".

A caution from the research itself: before 1633 there is no evidence of the painting or of any devotion to it, neither in the Parish Archive of Labateca nor in the colonial inspection records held at the National Archive of Colombia. What we know begins there.

1633: the chiefs found the confraternity

On 4 July 1633, eleven years after the visit of the royal judge Villabona, the chiefs and captains of the town of Labateca founded the confraternity of Our Lady of Sorrows — Nuestra Señora de las Angustias. It was approved by Bernardino de Almanza, Archbishop of the New Kingdom of Granada. By that year the Virgin was already considered the town's patron saint.

On paper, a confraternity was a lay religious brotherhood. In practice it was far more: the only institution in which the indigenous people of Labateca elected their own officers, managed common money, debated the town's affairs and made decisions. The town grew up around that image.

The list of founders names the chiefs and captains of the communities that had been brought together ten years earlier:

  • Don Andrés Sistiaba
  • Don Juan
  • Don Andrés Vergara
  • Don Alonso
  • Don Andrés
  • Don Lorenzo
  • Don Francisco
  • Don Cristóbal
  • Don Baltazar
  • Don Bartolomé
  • Juan Rosero

From Labateca, Balza, El Mariscal, Bochagá, Monega, Chona, Ima and Támara. Parish Archive of Labateca, Book of Confraternities 001.

The feast: three days and three processions

The Virgin's feast was the most important event of the year. It lasted three days, and the procession was divided among them: the first day for the Spaniards, the second for the native people of the town, and the third for outsiders. Each group elected its own alférez, or standard-bearer — the most prestigious office in the confraternity.

One detail deserves to be underlined. The offices of mayordoma and sacristana were reserved for women, and the sacristana was an indigenous woman. The mayordomas were likewise elected separately — Spanish, native and outsider — and were the female counterpart of the standard-bearers: they organised the feast and prepared the year's ceremonies alongside the priest, including the Nativity in December.

At first the priests viewed all this with suspicion. The celebrations resembled the old rites a little too closely, with a great deal of chicha involved. In 1620 the Archbishop of the New Kingdom had to send an order to the priests of Pamplona because residents were complaining that the feasts in the Indian towns turned into "great drunken revels" for a month before and a month after. Banners and drums were forbidden outside the church, and no more than one patronal feast a year was allowed.

It did not help much. Labateca's feasts became popular not only among the indigenous people but among mixed-race and Spanish residents who came from neighbouring towns and from Pamplona itself. In 1641 the Dominican friars wanted to found a confraternity of Our Lady of the Rosary here; the indigenous people asked that it be founded in the name of the one they already had, and the Dominicans agreed on one condition: that the feast of Las Angustias be held on 2 February.

And in 1657, a chief of the Bochagá community complained to the royal inspector about an encomendero with whom he had quarrelled over the noise of a sarao — a dance — that the indigenous people were rehearsing in the Virgin's honour.

How a brotherhood became the richest institution in town

Joining the confraternity cost money. From 1636 each indigenous member paid six reales — "of his own free will, without being pressed or forced" — outsiders paid eight, Black and mixed-race members one gold peso, and Spaniards two. Some Spaniards did not pay in cash: one friar joined in exchange for saying a mass; another, in exchange for preaching on the last day of the feast.

The confraternity owned land, a maize field that belonged to the Virgin, and above all a herd of cattle. When Don Joseph de Villamizar took over as steward in 1718, the confraternity owed thirty pesos to the priest, and what he was handed was half an arroba of wax and eighty head of cattle. By the time he died in 1758 he had turned it into one of the most important rural confraternities in the region. The inventory of 1742 already recorded 170 head of cattle, ornaments worth 238 pesos, a main chapel, a sacristy and a stone-and-lime shrine, and an organ that cost 550 pesos.

With that money the confraternity did something still visible today: it paid in full for the repair of the parish church throughout the whole of the 18th century.

1778: the procession that won a lawsuit

Late in the 18th century the Spanish Crown changed course. Arguing that the indigenous population had fallen sharply and no longer needed so much land, royal officials ordered the towns regrouped and the resguardos — the communal land reserves granted to indigenous communities — sold off to Spanish and mixed-race settlers. Of all the Indian towns left in the Pamplona district, only three were to remain: Servitá, Cácota and Chinácota.

The people of Labateca were to move to Cácota de Velasco. Losing the town, and losing the land.

Here is what they did: they carried the Virgin in procession for several days, invoking the favours they had received, and at the same time they fought the case before the royal courts, proving through the confraternity's accounts that the town could support itself. On 1 August 1778 the inspector Moreno y Escandón found that the confraternities' herd numbered 330 head of cattle, besides a communal maize field belonging to the Virgin.

A witness of the time left this description of that procession:

"There was not one person in that great multitude who did not weep… it was a theatre in which one saw a discreet confusion and an orderly labyrinth."

Memoirs of Father Burgos, prior of the convent of Santa Clara, quoted by Samuel Ramírez, Apuntes Históricos de Nuestra Señora de las Angustias, Pamplona, 1921.

They won. The people of Labateca kept their land, their own authorities and much of their customs. Labateca remained one of the last indigenous resguardos in the Santander region. They themselves gave two reasons: their faith in the Virgin, and the proven economic strength of the confraternity.

What remains

The resguardo did eventually disappear in the 19th century, when land privatisation laws put an end to the Indian towns and Labateca became a republican parish. But the image never moved.

Our Lady of Sorrows is still in the parish church of Labateca, the same one those indigenous people first saw on Friar Alba's cane frame. She has presided over the town square ever since, and her feast — now in November — is the great celebration of the municipality.

She is probably the oldest object in Labateca, and for once it is no exaggeration to say that the town exists because of her.
