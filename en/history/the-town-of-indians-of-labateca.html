THE TOWN OF INDIANS OF LABATECA

Encomienda, settlement and doctrine of the native population in the Valley of the Madmen. Research and text by Silvano Pabón Villamizar, historian (UIS). School of History, Universidad Industrial de Santander. San José de Cúcuta, Colombia.

I The pre-Hispanic population of the «Valley of the Madmen», sixteenth century

This wide stretch of the old province of Pamplona, known in Hispanic times as the «Valley of the Madmen» —so named by the first Spaniards who entered the area in 1550— was the seat of substantial indigenous communities of several peoples. The territory presented a complex ethnic convergence between the Chitarero peoples of the mountains and the U'wa-Tunebo groups, identified at the time under the geographical horizon of the Lache nation, settled in the low warm country of the Valegrá and lower Chitagá basins.

The Chitarero peoples, described by the colonial chroniclers as peaceable and easy to live alongside, occupied the high ground «more from the cold towards the warm», following the courses of rivers and streams. These groups made up the bulk of the population that could be granted in encomienda across this cluster of valleys, today the municipalities of Labateca and Toledo. Among them were the communities of Inegavita, Móluga, Chona, Mónega and Labateca, on the banks of the river Chitagá; Tecasquima, Balsa, El Mariscal, Culagá and Curagua, on the river Culagá; and the populous towns of Ima, Bochagá, Támara and their dependencies.

Recognised as settlements of Lache speech or U'wa-Tunebo origin were the towns of the Valegrá basin, the streams of Sopotá and Pedro Alonso, and the lower Chitagá, grouped under chiefdoms such as Sopotá and Cupagá. These Valegrá towns were administered from the doctrine of Servitá and never formed an integral part of Labateca: the inspectors and the ethnic authorities always argued their difference of language and custom.

Where the name «Valley of the Madmen» came from

It was the habit of the Spanish conquerors to name native places after some striking feature or after an anecdote of their campaigns. In Labateca's case, the name arose in 1550 when Pedro de Ursúa's men saw that the people of Ima and Labateca came out to meet them making «fierce show and great feeling, with grimaces and loud cries» — a ritual, gestural behaviour that the Europeans read as madness or savagery.

The shock of conquest and the resistance at Ima (1550–1551)

The Spanish occupation of the area was violent. Pedro de Ursúa and Ortún Velasco's expedition of 1550 subdued the communities by military force. The valley of Ima, which held a very dense settlement, was assaulted by the Spanish company. The records of the time —in particular the statement of the soldier Alonso Rodríguez de Escobar, made at Mompox before the judge Alonso de Zorita in June 1551— reveal under oath the cruelty of the campaign. Rodríguez de Escobar related how general Pedro de Ursúa led an attack on horseback, with men on foot and attack dogs, against the natives of the Ima valley, who tried to take shelter with their families in deep caves and ravines and were massacred and set upon by the dogs without any possibility of comparable armed resistance.

Despite the sharp demographic fall produced by the shock of conquest and the forced relocations that followed, the notary Cristóbal Bueno was able to record a total of 4,404 surviving tributary indigenous souls in the province of Pamplona at the first formal inspection of 1559. Modern historians estimate the valley's original pre-Hispanic population, before Spanish contact, at more than 10,000.

II The encomienda system and the working of the mines (1550–1600)

As soon as the city of Pamplona was founded in 1549, the soldiers of the conquering company received as reward for their service the «deposit» or allotment of the indigenous population in encomiendas. This colonial system consisted of a formal, theoretical act of possession before the Crown, followed by the actual subjection of the «able men» —married or single, between 18 and 50— to compulsory labour in the households, the farms and the rich mining works of the province.

The judge Tomás López's inspection of 1560

On 4 May 1560 the licentiate Tomás López, judge of the Royal Audiencia, arrived at the household of the encomendero Andrés de Acevedo in Labateca. With the help of an indigenous man named Juan, trained in Spanish, who served as interpreter, López questioned the local chief Batecabala in private.

Batecabala declared that no priest or doctrine friar had ever lived in his town, and that his own encomendero, Andrés de Acevedo, instructed and baptised them. But he reported that Acevedo constantly sent gangs of twenty indigenous boys to dig in his rich gold mines on the river Suratá and at La Quebradilla in the páramo of Las Vetas, and further obliged them to carry on their backs —for want of pack animals— heavy loads of maize to feed the miners. That testimony proved the systematic breach of His Majesty's provisions forbidding unpaid personal service and the carrying of heavy loads by indigenous people.

The agricultural and commercial boom of the late sixteenth century

The «Valley of the Madmen» quickly became one of the main farming, cattle and sugar enclaves of the province of Pamplona. Around 1574 Diego de Mora —successor and son-in-law of the conquistador Andrés de Acevedo, having married his daughter doña Catalina de Acevedo— became the region's pioneer in planting cane on a large scale and processing molasses, exporting «loaves or balls» of brown sugar as far as the ports of Ocaña and Cartagena.

The Serrano family's agricultural and cattle empire is magnificently illustrated in the will of Gonzalo Serrano —son of the conquistador Gonzalo Serrano Cortés— drawn up on 30 July 1590. Serrano declared that he owned ten holdings of cattle and of grain in Balsa and Ulagá, where he harvested wheat in the cold zones and cane in the temperate. His inventory recorded 250 breeding mares, 8 breeding donkeys and 800 sheep. This valuable document dispelled the traditional version put forward by the chronicler José de Jesús Rico, who held that Serrano had been beaten to death with war clubs by the rebel indigenous people of Ima.

III The legal institution of the reserves (1600–1623)

The policy of gathering the scattered indigenous population into towns planned in the Castilian manner —originally conceived by friar Bartolomé de las Casas— began to be formally applied in the province with the inspections of Tomás López in 1560, Melchor Vázquez Campuzano in 1583 and captain Alonso de Montalvo in 1586. In the Valley of Labateca, however, these provisions only came to life in 1602, with the exhaustive inspection of don Antonio Beltrán de Guevara, magistrate of Tunja.

Beltrán de Guevara fought the landowners' encroachment and heavily fined encomenderos such as captain Gonzalo de Salcedo, who was illegally occupying the best land of the Mónega reserve with his cane and maize fields, preventing the natives from working their own plots and forcing them to work without rest on Sundays, leaving them no time to attend the doctrine.

Guevara ordered the small or dying towns gathered into larger settlements, outlining the first formal reserves for Mónega, Chona and Móluga at the site of Locavita. To establish an autonomous economic order, he ordered each chief to keep ten hens and a cock, and each ordinary man six hens, for breeding and for the sale of eggs to meet their household needs.

How a doctrine chapel had to be built (1602)

As the centre of urban and civil life in the so-called «Republic of the Indians», Beltrán de Guevara decreed at Bochagá the strict architectural specifications under which the encomenderos were to build the doctrine chapels within three months, on pain of a fifty-peso gold fine:

  • <b>The nave.</b> Forty feet long and fifteen wide, built on stone foundations half a <i>vara</i> thick above ground, and three courses of rammed earth high, with adobe reinforcements and corners.
  • <b>Roof and doors.</b> A timber structure of fine local wood, roofed with well-tied thatch, whitewashed with lime inside, with a stout wooden door, key and bolt, and an outer porch with adobe benches.
  • <b>Furnishings and symbols.</b> Two stone basins —one for holy water, one for baptism—; an altar of adobe or rammed earth crowned with a canopy of cloth and a painted image; a side cupboard for the chrism vessels; a communal bronze bell to call the faithful; and a tall wooden cross before the main door.

IV The act of 1623: Labateca becomes a town

On 19 July 1623 doctor don Juan de Villabona y Zubiaurre, most senior judge of the Royal Audiencia and inspector general, issued at Labateca the definitive Act of Settlement. Villabona commissioned don Luis Jurado as settling magistrate and don Salvador de Ojeda as notary and surveyor, to gather and lay out fifteen allotments on a healthy temperate plain called Cananigua.

In his report of 19 August 1623, Luis Jurado stated that he had laid out the plaza and the streets precisely, building thirty-two houses —including the large thatched ones for the principal chiefs. Jurado admitted having delayed the allocation of individual farming plots in order to keep the indigenous people in the town and finish building their houses, since otherwise they fled to the hills. V The eighteenth century: the census of 1758

Through the eighteenth century, the ceaseless growth of the mestizo population and of white settlers attached around the reserve contrasted with the sharp fall of Labateca's indigenous population. On 23 October 1758 don Joaquín Aguilar y Quiroz, ordinary magistrate of Pamplona, working with the parish priest Ignacio Gereda —who provided the parish registers so that ages could be verified— drew up a meticulous roll of the surviving groups.

Read qualitatively, the census of 1758 reveals a deep crisis in the community's social cohesion: a high rate of family breakdown, a large number of single mothers (65 women) and a notable imbalance between the sexes. Despite holding their reserve lands, the original people were shrinking steadily as intermarriage and the pressure of the white <i>agregados</i> grew in the valley — a process that led in 1778 to the inspector Francisco Antonio Moreno y Escandón's proposal to extinguish the town of Labateca and move its people, with those of Silos, to Cácota de Velasco.

VI The parish separation and the birth of Toledo (1790–1796)

By the end of the eighteenth century, peaceful coexistence between the two populations had become unsustainable. The white and mestizo settlers of the valley, as <i>agregados</i> to the doctrine of the town of indians, were obliged to attend Labateca's small chapel to meet their sacramental duties.

In 1789 the landowners, represented by their attorney Clemente Robayo, formally petitioned the archbishopric for licence to erect an independent parish at the site of Belén. The petitioners based their case on the constant disputes and the mutual contempt between themselves and the natives of Labateca. The Spanish complained that during Holy Week and other festivals the indigenous people stole their belongings, deliberately damaged their planted fields, or loosed their horses at midnight in the town, causing serious discord.

Besides the civil quarrels, the white settlers advanced geographical and structural reasons. Labateca's church was far too narrow to hold both communities at once. They also argued the latent and imminent danger of ruin hanging over the church and the plaza of Labateca, built as they were on the edge of a very unstable bluff that in hard winters threatened to slide away.

The parish roll of 1790

Father Rafael Jácome y Guerrero's measurement

On 22 February 1790 the commissioned cleric don Rafael Jácome y Guerrero certified, after inspection, that the church of Labateca was indeed far too small for the number of indigenous people and neighbours attending services. Jácome measured the straight-line distance between the site of Belén and the main plaza of Labateca, certifying under oath that it was three thousand seven hundred and fifty (3,750) <i>varas</i>, with a rough river between the two settlements —the Culagá— that in winter became impassable.

On 20 January 1796, after years of dispute and after doña Juana Gertrudis de Almeyda —widow of Tomás de Mora, a descendant of Labateca's encomenderos— formally donated the Toledo pasture at the site of El Salado, the definitive royal title of the new parish of Our Lady of Sorrows of Toledo was signed by order of the viceroy and royal vice-patron Josef de Ezpeleta.

The line that finally separated the two parishes followed the course of the river Culagá from its head at the Serrano stream, downstream to its meeting with the Chitagá — serving as the natural and institutional frontier between the town of indians of Labateca and the new parish of Toledo.
