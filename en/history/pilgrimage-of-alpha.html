A NINETEENTH-CENTURY HISTORICAL AND GEOGRAPHICAL CHRONICLE

«La Peregrinación de Alpha» — the journey of the Chorographic Commission through north-eastern New Granada, 1850–1851.

Original author: Manuel Ancízar, writing as «Alpha», secretary of Agustín Codazzi's Chorographic Commission. Sources compiled and edited by the historical project «Labateca, Volcanes de Dios».

Editor's foreword · the Chorographic Commission in Santander

The Chorographic Commission, conceived under the presidency of Tomás Cipriano de Mosquera and formally established in 1850 under José Hilario López, was the most ambitious scientific and territorial survey of nineteenth-century Colombia. Led by the Italian engineer and geographer colonel Agustín Codazzi, its purpose was to draw the country's map and to assess its natural resources, its people and its routes of communication.

In the province of Pamplona the Commission took on Manuel Ancízar as historian, describer and secretary. Ancízar, born in 1811, was a notable intellectual, journalist and politician, and founder of the newspaper «El Neo-granadino». Out of his passage through the rough valleys of north-eastern Colombia came «La Peregrinación de Alpha», a work that joins the precision of a travel diary to the sharpness of a sociological and geographical analysis. This volume brings together the chronicles that cover the Canton of Salazar, the valleys of Cúcuta and the province of Pamplona in 1850 and 1851.

<i>A note on this edition:</i> the passages gathered here are strictly those of Ancízar's chronicles that form part of the historiographical sources of the municipality of Labateca. On the Spanish page the original spelling of the period and its nineteenth-century geographical descriptions are preserved untouched; here they are given in English translation.

I The Canton of Salazar de las Palmas

The Canton of Salazar de las Palmas took in a wide stretch of Andean slope in the north-east of New Granada. Ancízar describes its territory with admiration for its natural wealth and its climate, but also with a critical eye on how far behind its roads and its schools had fallen.

Father Romero and the coffee revolution (1834–1851)

Ancízar singles out the origin of Salazar's agricultural prosperity, and puts it down directly to an ingenious piece of church initiative that began in 1834:

«In 1834 father Romero came to Salazar as parish priest, and by example, by exhortation and by the penances he set in the confessional, he got the townspeople to plant coffee trees, which prosper there admirably — the bushes are to be seen loaded at once with flower, green fruit and ripe cherry, so that the harvest never ends. So much did he do, helped by Mr Fraser, a veteran of the independence, that in 1851 the canton of Salazar contributed 6,000 hundredweight of coffee to Santander's exports, taking in close to 80,000 pesos in exchange for this precious bean, shared out among a multitude of smallholders who today bless the memory of their good parish priest, to whom they truly owe the growing well-being they enjoy.»

That formidable penance —planting coffee trees for every sin, mortal or venial, and in particular for every act of fornication— became one of the most effective civilising and productive forces in the territory. It not only drove the farming vocation of the hills of Salazar and Chinácota; it shaped a coffee «golden triangle» in which San José de Cúcuta acted as the great river port of collection and export.

The geography and population of the town of Salazar

Ancízar describes the town of Salazar de las Palmas in these terms:

«Salazar, set on the left bank of the river of the same name, a tributary of the Zulia, which lies three leagues to the east of it, enjoys a benign temper and a healthy climate: the centigrade thermometer marks a minimum of 20° and a maximum of 25°, and the seat of the town stands 852 metres above sea level. The district numbers 4,631 inhabitants, in possession of a fertile and cheerful country very favourable to agriculture, which is varied, though not as rich in its products as the soil would allow. The inhabitants are white and mestizo, healthy people, of the best disposition.»

In his geographical analysis Ancízar adds that the Canton of Salazar was made up of the head district and those of Arboleda, Santiago and San Cayetano, nominally occupying 159 square leagues, of which 49 were settled and 110 remained empty, with 8,300 inhabitants in all.

II Arboledas and the school census of 1850

On his journey through the province, Ancízar made an excursion north to the village of Arboledas, on the mountainous edge of Pamplona. He describes the road there:

«Arboleda lies three and six-tenths leagues from Salazar by a road barely traced, but crossing most beautiful country along the left bank of the Zulia, abundantly watered and clothed in fine and varied forest. The village is old, but small and thatched, and it deserves the name it carries, being built in the midst of stands of leafy trees. Its river runs close by, breaking noisily over the stones and cooling the air.»

The paradox of the schools

For all the imposing beauty of the landscape and the mineral wealth of the area —sheets of bright mica abound nearby— Ancízar sets out with real dismay the state of social and educational neglect in which the children of Arboledas were living:

«There are, by the latest census, 536 children of both sexes of an age to go to school, and only 15 boys are learning to shout the primer and scrawl in sand.»

That desolating figure was a recurring pattern in the region. The commercial and landed elites concentrated their efforts on the export trade and on enlarging their coffee and cacao estates, leaving the instruction of children and young people almost entirely unattended — which drew hard criticism from the Commission's secretary.

III Santiago and San Cayetano · tolls and burning heat

The Commission went on towards the lower banks of the rivers Peralonso and Zulia, visiting the districts of Santiago and San Cayetano, which mule trains loaded with coffee had no choice but to pass through:

«Three and a half leagues north-east of Salazar lies Santiago, on the banks of the river Peralonso, and three leagues beyond, on the Zulia, is San Cayetano — villages of burning temperature, decimated by fevers and threatened with ruin by the enormous tolls that the council of the latter has imposed on every load passing through, taking advantage of there being no other road to communicate with San José.»

How the trade answered

Ancízar condemns the ignorance of the San Cayetano council, explaining that such tolls harassed free traffic and brought about the ruin of the village's own prosperity. He describes what the producers of the province did about it:

«The producers of Salazar and Santiago resolved to shake off the yoke and not to pass through the covetous parish: they traced a new road near the Zulia and threw a bridge over the river to carry their loads toll-free to San José. San Cayetano, a village that does not know what a primary school is, already suffers the penalty of being cut off from the rest, and it would be no great matter if it ended its obscure existence by disappearing from among the districts.»

Leaving Salazar, the Commission met the governor of the province, don Isidro Villamizar, whom Ancízar praises as «the very type of honesty and of ingenuous, tireless patriotism». The governor guided them along the new road on a hard night journey, in which they had to shelter in a hut surrounded by a neglected cacao wood belonging to the nuns of Pamplona — that is, to «dead hands», which were letting the former owner's production wither.

IV San José de Cúcuta and the northern frontier

The chronicle of San José de Cúcuta, capital of the province of Santander, is rich in geographical and meteorological detail, and describes a commercial centre of great energy:

«San José, capital of the province of Santander, had its origin at the beginning of the last century in a few huts attached to the nearby parish of Cúcuta. Around the year 1734 it formed a separate curacy under the name of San José del Guasimal, and by 1792 it was so prosperous that it obtained the title of town, dropping the name Guasimal for that of Cúcuta, in memory of its origin. Finally the legislature of 1850 created this province and designated the town of San José as the seat of government. It stands 294 metres above sea level, on the left bank of the Pamplonita and on a sandy plain, ringed by barren hills.»

The climate of the Cúcuta valley

Ancízar analyses the climate of the valley with a meteorologist's rigour, explaining how the air currents keep the fevers from settling:

«The temperature ranges between 21° centigrade, which is that of November to February, and 32° in the hot season, from July to October; strong south-easterly winds, produced by the rarefaction of the air over the great basin of Lake Maracaibo, stir the atmosphere during the other months and bring the temperature down to 25°, which in calm rises to 31°… The climate is therefore thoroughly burning, and would be unhealthy but for those winds, which drive off the miasmas raised on the wooded banks of the Táchira and the Zulia — deadly places even for those accustomed to them.»

The dances of Cúcuta

The most celebrated passage of the chronicle describes, in a few poetic strokes, the popular dances of the Cúcuta valleys —the «Maromera», the «Payasa» and the «Relámpago»— where the petticoats swing under the light of tallow candles set in paper cones:

«… a young man comes to the middle of the ring in his shirtsleeves, arms crossed behind his back, and begins to stamp out the rhythm alone, showing off his person and his skill… one of the women steps forward, greets him, and takes up rapid circling turns without any movement of the feet appearing, holding open with both hands the wide printed petticoat to show the embroidered hem of the white underskirts… One presents herself: she is the beauty in fashion… She wears a fine chemise with embroidery and lace, cut very low at the back and somewhat less in front, pressed and drawn by the drawstring of an organdie petticoat, tight at the slender waist and falling in folds over the moving curves of the body; she wears small cloth shoes, and her head is adorned with a little nacuma-palm hat with a fine ribbon, letting the abundant hair be seen behind, gathered and held by a thick enamelled pin; long gold earrings swing sounding against her uncovered neck…»

He describes as well the gloomy contrast of the gaming tables, presided over by the impassive keeper, where the chiaroscuro of the tallow candles picks out the tense faces of unlettered farmhands and the drama of what is lost in a night.

The northern canton · San Faustino and Limoncito

Ancízar describes the precarious frontier of the northern canton towards the river La Grita and the Venezuelan border, noting how tropical fevers had emptied the forests:

«North of San José and as far as the river La Grita… the canton's territory extends over 50 square leagues covered in forests dangerous to health, dense and deserted, for there are only two villages: Limoncito, on the banks of the Zulia to the west, its district counting 368 inhabitants, and San Faustino, near the Táchira to the east, with 544 — the wretched remnant of the city founded in the year 1662 by Antonio de los Ríos Jimeno, depopulated by fevers and its rich cacao plantations ruined.»

To remedy this commercial backwardness, Ancízar strongly recommends building a new cart road direct from San José de Cúcuta to the meeting of the rivers Zulia and Táchira —Puerto Santander, Bocas del Grita— which would allow the northern wastes to be settled and would raise the Republic's trade and customs revenue beyond calculation.
