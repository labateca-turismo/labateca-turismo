SETTLEMENT, ORIGINS AND INSTITUTIONS OF THE HUMAN SETTLEMENTS OF NORTE DE SANTANDER

The origin and historical development of San José de Cúcuta: from the indigenous reserve of 1641 to the coffee export economy.

A historiographical compilation of the original texts of Silvano Pabón Villamizar, historian (UIS), documentalist and archivist. Mayor's Office of San José de Cúcuta — Bicentenary edition.

I The five legal moments of settlement

The settlement of what is today Norte de Santander was neither chaotic nor spontaneous. It was a systematic process running across more than four centuries, and it can be understood through five great moments, each defined by precise legal and institutional categories that explain how every settlement came to be — first under Spanish colonial law, and later under the Colombian republic.

«The projects of conquest, agreed and contracted under a triple and mixed legal condition, arose from private initiative, were financed with private capital, but were legalised and legitimised by the sovereign Power of the King through the capitulations, and were carried out under the military structure of his companies.» (Pabón Villamizar, 2015)

1.1 · The five legal forms of occupying the land

The settlement of the north-eastern Colombian Andes falls under five political and administrative formulas, and each of them changed the landscape radically.

<b>The moment of the cities (1538–1662).</b> Its verb is <i>to found</i>. It meant the military transformation of the conquering companies into civil councils, so that the King's justice could be exercised. Pamplona (1549), Ocaña (1570), Salazar de las Palmas (1583) and the frontier city and governorship of San Faustino de los Ríos (1662) were born at this stage.

<b>The moment of the towns of indians (seventeenth century).</b> Its verb is <i>to settle</i>. Inspired by the doctrines of Las Casas, it sought to gather the native population into settlements laid out on a grid, «in the manner of Spain», marking out a reserve of common land and raising a doctrine chapel. In this century Silos, Chopo, <b>Labateca</b>, Aspasica, Chinácota and the Town of Indians of Cúcuta (1641) were institutionalised.

<b>The moment of the diocesan parishes (eighteenth century).</b> Its verb is <i>to erect</i>. It came in answer to rapid intermarriage and to the growing number of white and mestizo settlers who established themselves as <i>agregados</i> —attached households— on the edges of the indigenous communal lands. Through deeds guaranteeing the parish priest's income, parishes such as San Joseph del Guasimal (1734), Nuestra Señora del Rosario (1761), Bochalema (1757) and Santa Bárbara del Llano de la Cruz (Ábrego, 1807) came into being.

<b>The moment of the coffee towns (nineteenth century).</b> Driven by the introduction and export of the bean to world markets. It meant the settlement of mountain slopes until then uncultivated, the opening of mule roads, and the birth of coffee municipalities such as Galindo (Gramalote, 1864), San Calixto (1845), San Pedro (Villa Caro, 1857), Cáchira (1866) and La Playa de Belén (1896).

<b>The moment of the contemporary and frontier towns (twentieth century).</b> These grew alongside the major roads, the railway, oil extraction or the expanding agricultural frontier: El Zulia (1959), Los Patios, Puerto Santander (1993), and the oil stronghold of Tibú, which arose in the heart of the Catatumbo out of the Barco Concession.

II The Spanish entry into the Cúcuta valley

The Cúcuta valley was formally brought into the Spanish empire after the founding of Pamplona on 2 November 1549 by Ortún Velasco and Pedro de Ursúa. The conquering company, turned into a civil council, drew an immense jurisdiction reaching from the basin of the river Chicamocha to the forests of the Catatumbo, the plains of Venezuela and Lake Maracaibo.

From the 1550s the council of Pamplona granted the first land titles on the plains of Cúcuta, setting this wide flat valley aside as common grazing for the breeding of horses, mules and cattle — indispensable for transport in the Andes. But the formal settlement of Spaniards and indigenous people was marked from the start by the neighbouring rivalry of the town of San Cristóbal, founded in 1561 by captain Juan de Maldonado.

2.1 · The ambiguity of the river Cúcuta

When San Cristóbal was founded, its provisional boundaries were marked with the river Cúcuta —today the Pamplonita— as the natural dividing line between the two municipalities. That delimitation produced a long history of absent control, a strong attachment to illegality, contraband and tax evasion among the valley's first inhabitants, who declared themselves subjects of the council of Pamplona or of the town of San Cristóbal according to whichever was more convenient for their taxes at the time.

III The Town of Indians of Cúcuta and its doctrine (1641)

The ecclesiastical and civil origin of the Cúcuta valley lies in the Town of Indians of Cúcuta, formally erected as a doctrine and communal reserve on 2 December 1641 by the judge and inspector general don Diego de Carrasquilla Maldonado. Before that act the indigenous people lived scattered, subject to the encomienda system under men such as Sebastián Lorenzo and Pedro Dionisio Velasco.

Carrasquilla marked out the reserve lands on the right bank of the river Pamplona —today the San Luis neighbourhood— gathering at the site of los Camaracos the native groups of the Cúcutas, Tamacos, Camaracos, Abricaes and Casaderos. The reserve was placed under the doctrine priests of the Order of Saint Augustine, from the convent of San Cristóbal, and so became the oldest municipality and curacy in the territory.

IV The erection of the parish of San Joseph de Guasimal (1734)

The unstoppable growth of the mestizo and mulatto farming population, and the arrival of white landowners in the eighteenth century, put the reserve lands of the indigenous doctrine under constant pressure. This growing mass of white neighbours had no urban rights inside the Town of Indians of Cúcuta, and lived in the uncomfortable and precarious political condition of <i>agregados</i>.

To claim a political status of their own and to establish a secular diocesan curacy, the local elites —led by Juan Jacinto de Colmenares, Juan de Lara Jovel and Félix Trujillo— held a series of meetings at the Tonchalá estate. The case was formalised on 17 June 1733, the day the matron Juana Rangel de Cuéllar signed the deed donating half a cattle holding, about 782 hectares, at the site of Guasimal, bounded by the Lara Jovel irrigation ditch and the Jesuits' estate.

«As regards ecclesiastical jurisdiction, we erect into a parish and new benefice the site of Guasimal, segregated from that of the town of Cúcuta, in view of the parish priest's income being secured by mortgages of four thousand patacones signed by twenty-eight founding neighbours.» (Act of erection, archbishop Antonio Claudio Álvarez de Quiñónez, 13 November 1734)

The ecclesiastical approval was ratified in civil law by the president of the Royal Audiencia and royal vice-patron, don Rafael de Eslava, on 20 November 1734, ordering the plaza of San Joseph to be marked out and the church of post-and-wattle and the public gaol to be raised — formally separating the neighbours from the indigenous curacy.

V The «most noble, valiant and loyal town» of San José de Cúcuta (1792–1793)

By the end of the eighteenth century, the boom in cacao plantations had turned the parish of San Joseph de Guasimal into a dynamic triangle of wealth together with the port of Los Cachos on the river Zulia. That demographic and fiscal standing led its leading men to ask king Charles IV for separation from the council of Pamplona and promotion to the status of town.

Charles IV issued the royal decree granting the title of «Most noble, valiant and loyal town of San José de Cúcuta» on 18 May 1792, in recognition of its agricultural growth and of the military fervour of its inhabitants in defending royal sovereignty against the Comuneros insurrection of 1781.

5.1 · Marking out the town's common lands (26 April 1793)

To put the new civil authorities in possession, viceroy José de Ezpeleta commissioned the lieutenant magistrate of Pamplona, don Juan Antonio Villamizar. On 26 April 1793, with the surveyor Miguel Chacón and don Luis Ignacio Santander acting as civil trustee, the 782 hectares of common land donated by Juana Rangel were measured with cords and marked out. The survey certified a length of 3,112 Castilian <i>varas</i> from the Lara Jovel ditch to the ram's lookout, by a width of 617 <i>varas</i>, settling the disputes with the hospital's neighbours.

VI The coffee miracle (nineteenth century)

The new century opened with the biological devastation of the cacao estates, wiped out by the blight known as <i>la mancha</i>. But the Andean slopes of the Pamplonita and the Zulia found a brilliant replacement: coffee. Planting spread swiftly through Salazar, Chinácota, Gramalote and Pamplona, making the square of San José the hub of collection and export towards the port of Maracaibo.

That commercial drive attracted a strong presence of foreign trading houses —mostly German— such as Van Dissel & Thies, Minlos Breuer & Cía. and Andressen Moller & Cía., which financed the local coffee growers and brought in new technology. To move the crop, the San Buenaventura cart road was built and, after the earthquake of 1875, the monumental Cúcuta Railway, which connected with the Táchira and cut transport times to a few days by river.

«San José is a place of commerce and the centre of a mercantile movement that in 1851 reached 10,720,627 reales in exports and imports registered by the customs house, with a volume of trade sustained by more than a thousand mules and by a neighbourhood of hard-working foreigners.» (Manuel Ancízar, La Peregrinación de Alpha, 1851)

VII What Cúcuta has been called, 1773 to today

The legal and institutional outline of San José de Cúcuta reflects the successive territorial transformations of the Colombian state, from Bourbon rule in the colony to its consolidation as the definitive capital of Norte de Santander in 1910.
