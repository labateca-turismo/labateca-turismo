«América Dolor Inédito»: a note of contrast

A critical study of the work by José de Jesús Rico Villamizar (1992)

Compiled and presented by José Rafael Cáceres Garavito

Three historiographical debates where a well-loved local book and the archive research disagree: the Carib origin of the Valley's peoples, the death of the encomendero Gonzalo Serrano, and the alliances of the conquest frontier.

This is a research note, not the work itself. «América Dolor Inédito» runs to more than 500 pages and is not digitised; it is not reproduced here. What is published is the study made of it for «Labateca: Volcanes de Dios»: its arguments, the pages cited, and what the archive confirms or contradicts.

RESEARCH NOTE AND HISTORIOGRAPHICAL CONTRAST

A critical study of the work «América Dolor Inédito» by José de Jesús Rico Villamizar (Editorial Antares, Bogotá, 1992).

Compiled, presented and offered by Ing. José Rafael Cáceres Garavito — a gift of filial love and technological preservation for my beloved town of Labateca. Tricentenary Historiographical Collection, Labateca, Norte de Santander, 2026.

What this note is for

This note sets out, once and for all, the place that «América Dolor Inédito» occupies among the historical sources for the municipality of Labateca and the old province of Pamplona.

The work is an active reference in this project. The full text of the book —more than 500 pages in the original— is not loaded as a separate file here, for the plain reason that it has not been digitised. But its content, its demographic arguments, its chronicles of war and the points where it parts company with the notarial research of the historian Silvano Pabón Villamizar have all been worked through, from the bibliographic survey and from the critical debates built into the primary sources.

The work, in brief

Critical analysis: three debates

The point of bringing Rico Villamizar's «América Dolor Inédito» into this collection is not to accept it as settled truth, but its value for historiographical contrast. Setting Rico's lyrical, traditional arguments beside Silvano Pabón's rigorous archival research brings out three large historical debates, and they make the book richer.

Debate I · Carib origin, or Chitarero-Tunebo?

• Rico's thesis (1992). Rico Villamizar holds (pp. 488 ff.) that the indigenous groups of the Valley of Labateca —specifically the peoples of Ima, Támara and the fierce Gemarás— had a clear Carib ethnic ancestry. He grounds this in their extremely warlike and hostile conduct towards Pedro de Ursúa's invading company in 1550, linking them directly with the bellicose Chicaguaos of the nearby valley of Arboledas. • Pabón's counter-argument. Pabón Villamizar firmly rejects that historiographical stereotype of a warlike Carib character. Drawing on the inspections carried out by the Crown's judges, he shows that the basin of the rivers Culagá, Bochagá and Chitagá was in fact a zone of peaceful convergence and transition between the industrious Chitarero peoples of the mountains —linguistically and culturally related to the Muisca highlands— and the U'wa-Tuneba and Lache communities of the páramo slopes. Rico's chronicles of war therefore read as natural bellicosity what was in truth a harsh and desperate act of self-defence against the extreme military cruelty of the Spanish soldiers in the massacres of 1550.

Debate II · The myth of Gonzalo Serrano's death (1590)

• Rico's thesis (1992). In a dramatic passage of «América Dolor Inédito» (p. 513), the author tells, in novelistic fashion, that the wealthy encomendero don Gonzalo Serrano —son of the conquistador Gonzalo Serrano Cortés— died tragically under the war clubs of the rebel indigenous people of Ima, in direct reprisal for the whippings and physical humiliations he constantly inflicted on their chief. According to Rico, Serrano's body was then cut up and left in pieces along the royal roads. • Pabón's counter-argument. Research in primary archival sources flatly disproves this popular legend. Silvano Pabón located and transcribed don Gonzalo Serrano's original will and testament, drawn up at his sickbed in Pamplona on 30 July 1590 (Historical Archive of Pamplona, Notarial Protocol, vol. 6, ff. 101r–106v). The will proves that Serrano was in full possession of his faculties, writing a long and detailed document in which he declared he owned cattle and wheat holdings in Balsa and Ulagá, left his goods to his lawful heirs, and died peacefully of natural causes in his bed in Pamplona — not at the hands of an indigenous attack.

Debate III · Frontier alliances and the Socarima case

• Where Rico adds something. Not everything in Rico's work is mythical imprecision; the author documents with real acuity an event of singular ethnohistorical value: the opportune alliances between peoples that arose on the frontier of conquest. Rico (p. 248) describes how the indigenous people of Socarima received Pedro de Ursúa's Spanish soldiers as liberators and heroes, offering them ransom gold, food and labour so that they would intervene on their side in a long-running territorial and military conflict with their neighbours in other parts of the Valley. That passage, fully corroborated by the chronicles of friar Pedro de Aguado, shows that the pattern of indigenous subjection was not uniform, and was mediated by the subtle geopolitical interests of the native chiefs themselves.

Editorial conclusion

Including «América Dolor Inédito» in this historiographical collection is an exercise in intellectual maturity. It allows the finished book, «Labateca: Volcanes de Dios», not to limit itself to repeating the district's popular myths, but to examine them scientifically — tracing where the colonial legends came from and confirming the town's real history through the rigour of archival evidence and documentary truth.
