THE COFFEE ESTATES AND REGIONAL HISTORIOGRAPHY

A critical study of agrarian production in the Province of Pamplona and an analysis of the historiographical sources of friar Andrés Mezanza and don Luis Eduardo Páez Courvel.

Compiled, unified and presented by Ing. José Rafael Cáceres Garavito — a gift of filial love and technological preservation for my beloved town of Labateca.

Introduction

Reconstructing the settlements and economic dynamics of north-eastern Colombia calls for a rigorous cross-reading of primary and secondary sources. The basin of the river Zulia and the Valley of Sorrows —places where Andean mysticism meets the drive of the land— have historically been defined by their patterns of settlement, their agrarian crises and the later arrival of export crops. This study sets out to examine critically three of the most significant pillars of that regional historiography: the development of the economy and the coffee estates in the valleys of Cúcuta and Táchira, analysed in the research of the historian Silvano Pabón Villamizar; the validation of the local Marian heritage carried out by the reverend father friar Andrés Mezanza; and the early encomienda system in the Province of Pamplona, documented in classic form by don Luis Eduardo Páez Courvel.

Through this examination it becomes clear how the processes of territorial hispanisation, the formation of indigenous brotherhoods with real financial standing, and the subsequent shift from a single-crop cacao export model to a coffee one changed the physical, cultural and social geography of the Valley of Labateca irreversibly. This effort of academic compilation stands as a teaching tool: to safeguard the memory of the territory and to give scientific rigour to the accounts of its colonial and republican past.

1 The coffee estates in the valleys of Cúcuta and Táchira

The economic transition that north-eastern Colombia went through during the nineteenth century is a turning point in its settlement and its spatial construction. Historically, the fertile banks of the rivers Zulia, Táchira and Pamplonita had been the seat of immense, thriving cane fields and cacao plantations of more than 50,000 fruit trees, forming a dense export enclave from the seventeenth century onwards. By the middle of the nineteenth century, however, the cacao groves suffered the devastating blows of the blight known as «la mancha» or «ceniza», a plant disease that destroyed whole harvests and stripped the region's estate owners of their capital. Faced with that crisis, growers found in coffee the saving crop that could restore the district's finances.

As the rigorous research of the historian Silvano Pabón Villamizar shows —against the widespread schoolbook legend that places the origin of the bean solely in Rubio, Venezuela, or in Salazar de las Palmas— coffee first entered and took hold on a commercial scale in the lowlands of the river Táchira, within the jurisdiction of Villa del Rosario. Agricultural inventories and notarial deeds prove that as early as 1803 and 1809, pioneering growers such as don Antonio Sánchez Osorio and don Pedro Chauveau were processing and exporting coffee alongside cacao.

«La crisis en la producción cacaotera dejó diezmado el capital de los cultivadores y paralizados los negocios de las Casas Comerciales exportadoras en toda la Región Zuliana. Esta situación la salvó el espíritu emprendedor de los cultivadores y la prontitud con que buscaron otro producto que tuviera igual o mayor demanda en los mercados internacionales: el café.»

The definitive entry of the Zulia basin and the Andean slopes of Salazar, Gramalote, Arboledas, Durania and Labateca into the international market came through what has been called the «Maracaibo export circuit». The city of San José de Cúcuta acted as the commercial hub and collection centre for the output of the whole of north-eastern Colombia and the border Andes, connecting the bean to the great markets of London, Hamburg and New York by way of the river Zulia and the port of Maracaibo.

The financial structure that carried this expansion rested on the large German and foreign trading houses —Van Dissel, Thies & Cía., Minlos Breuer & Cía., Andressen Moller & Cía., and Spannocchia Ferrero & Cía. These firms replaced the old ecclesiastical credit of the censo and set up financing by pledging or selling the harvest in advance, the so-called «ida y retorno» system. Under that model, smallholding farmers drew food, cloth, footwear and farm tools on credit through the year, undertaking to hand over their coffee at the end of the harvest, with accounts settled at the prices set on the international exchanges.

In terms of labour, and unlike the slavery and colonial servitude that prevailed in the cane fields, mountain coffee growing was marked by the predominance of small and medium family holdings. On the larger estates, purely commercial arrangements were adopted —«aparcería» (sharecropping) and «contratos de compañía» or halves, where the owner and the sharecropper shared the costs of picking and split the harvest between them— producing a significantly more equal Andean rural society.

2 Friar Andrés Mezanza and the Marian historiography of Labateca

The spiritual dimension and the mysticism of the Valley of Sorrows rest documentarily not only on the sworn testimony of the elders and chiefs of 1790 —such as the well-known statement of the ninety-year-old indigenous man Tomás Bochagá— but on the works of the great chroniclers of the Catholic Church in Colombia. Among these first-order historiographical references stands out the mention made by the reverend father friar Andrés Mezanza of the Order of Preachers (O.P.) in his monumental work «Célebres Imágenes y Santuarios de Nuestra Señora en Colombia», published in the 1920s.

Father Mezanza, known for cataloguing with scientific and historical rigour the oldest and most influential Marian devotions in the country, devotes a prominent place to the image of Our Lady of Sorrows of Labateca. In his work he confirms and celebrates the antiquity and the widespread piety surrounding the sacred canvas, listing it as one of the oldest shrines and the most visited and best loved in the whole department of Norte de Santander — which set a seal of ecclesiastical and institutional legitimacy on the devotion of the people of Labateca.

«El santuario de Nuestra Señora de las Angustias de Labateca es uno de los más antiguos de Colombia y el más visitado y amado que tiene el departamento Norte de Santander, como muy bien lo asegura el Rvdo. Padre Fr. Andrés Mezanza en su obra, 'Célebres Imágenes y Santuarios de Ntra. Sra. en Colombia', cuya simpática devoción ha ido corrigiendo los ámbitos de nuestra fe.»

Mezanza's confirmation also recovers the background to the appearance of the canvas. According to the Book of Brotherhoods of 1730 and the records reconstructed by Fr. Samuel Ramírez in 1921, the Marian image was found in 1560 in the waters of the Bochagá lagoon by the indigenous woman María Berbesí and her husband Juan Romualdo Bateca. When they tried to wash the canvas, it miraculously revealed the suffering face of the Virgin and the sorrows of the Passion. Handed to the Dominican doctrine priest friar Francisco de Alba, the canvas spiritually united the indigenous communities and became, from the founding of its brotherhood on 4 July 1633, the chief shield of ethnic resistance and of the defence of the reserve's communal lands against the pressure of Spanish landowners.

3 Luis Eduardo Páez Courvel and the encomienda system in Pamplona

To trace with rigour the demographic and territorial evolution of the Valley of Labateca, it is essential to turn to the studies of the distinguished Ocaña-born historian don Luis Eduardo Páez Courvel (1908–1950). His classic works «Los Fundadores de Pamplona» (1941) and «Las Encomiendas de Pamplona» (published in the 1940s in the Boletín de Historia y Antigüedades) are the scholarly foundation for tracking the Spanish occupation of the Sierras Nevadas and the allotment and assessment of the Chitarero people subjected to labour tribute.

Páez Courvel documents in detail how the Valley of Labateca and its upper basin —called by the conquerors the «Valley of the Madmen» because of the desperate resistance of its inhabitants— were incorporated into the Province of Pamplona immediately after the city was founded on 1 November 1549. The captain general don Pedro de Ursúa proceeded to deposit and parcel out the local people in encomiendas to the first soldiers of his company, in order to secure labour for the mining districts and for the development of farming and cattle holdings.

Through the land inspections examined by Páez Courvel and Germán Colmenares —the first census by the notary Cristóbal Bueno in June 1559, which recorded 4,404 souls subject in the Valley of Labateca, and the visit of the licentiate Tomás López in May 1560— the structure of early land tenure in the basin becomes clear. The most influential encomenderos in the territory were don Gonzalo Serrano (holder of Balsa and Ima, who ran a thriving livestock operation with 250 mares and 800 sheep, sowing wheat in the cold country and cane in the temperate), don Andrés de Acevedo and his successor don Diego de Mora (who developed the first mills and sugar works for the export of sugar loaves to Cartagena), don Juan de Toloza (in Móluga and Leuta) and don Nicolás de Palencia (in Chucarima and Sopotá).

Páez Courvel's historiographical analysis shows that it was precisely on this base of the sixteenth-century encomienda system that the following centuries drew the Act of Settlement of Cananigua by the judge Juan de Villabona y Zubiaurre in 1623, the later erection of the parish for free people in 1719, and the growth of the white and mestizo farming population that shaped the agrarian identity of modern Labateca.
