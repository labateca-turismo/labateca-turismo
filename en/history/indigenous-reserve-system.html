Compiled and analysed by José Rafael Cáceres Garavito

How the communal lands of Labateca's indigenous community were created, defended and finally lost — and why the brotherhood's accounts were what defeated the Crown's order to erase the town in 1778.

THE INDIGENOUS RESERVE SYSTEM IN SANTANDER (1993)

A critical study and historiographical reconstruction of the law, the workings, the resistance and the dispossession of indigenous communal lands in Labateca and the province of Pamplona.

Compiled, analysed and presented by Ing. José Rafael Cáceres Garavito — Collection for the preservation of the historical memory of Labateca.

Dedication

To the living memory and the honour of my father, don José Eugenio Cáceres Suárez, who as mayor led the fate of our municipality with deep faith and civic spirit, leaving his signature on the sacred «vow of filial love» to our Dark Virgin of the Valley. This study of the origin, the protection and the defence of the communal lands of our indigenous ancestors of Labateca is my own vow of faith and of technological preservation. A gift for the minds and the souls of all my fellow townspeople.

Foreword

The history of property in our Andean region of Norte de Santander is a chronicle of constant siege and of admirable resilience. In my daily work as an electronic engineer, rigour, the precision of systems and the analysis of data are what make any design better. But turning my eyes to the past of my beloved town, Labateca, I have come to understand that human societies also obey structures and laws of great precision — and that the indigenous reserve was the main gear of ethnic survival against the blows of the colonial system and the expansion of the landowners.

The work of the respected historian Armando Martínez Garnica, «El Régimen del Resguardo en Santander» (1993), is a landmark in our historiography. In it, Martínez Garnica unpicks the legal scaffolding of the eighteenth-century Bourbon Reforms, showing how the Spanish Crown set out to extinguish the towns of indians and auction their lands to the growing white and mestizo peasantry. Labateca stands out in that work: it was one of the very few reserves in north-eastern Colombia that managed to resist dissolution, and it did so thanks to the remarkable financial standing and political organisation of its Brotherhood of Our Lady of Sorrows.

Putting this study within reach of the people of Labateca, in a clean, rigorous and easily downloadable form, is my contribution as a man of science to preserving the intellectual heritage of the Valley of Sorrows. Let us know our inheritance: it is the only way to face our future with pride and dignity.

Ing. José Rafael Cáceres Garavito

I The legal framework of the «Republic of the Indians»

The establishment of Spanish sovereignty over the Andean lands of the old province of Pamplona brought a deep change in how land was held and owned. After the resounding failure of the protective laws of 1542, which had sought to moderate the abuses of personal service and to regulate tribute, the Spanish Crown designed in the last decades of the sixteenth century an ambitious project of territorial reordering. That model rested on the well-known theoretical division of the «two republics»: the Republic of the Spaniards and the Republic of the Indians.

The indigenous reserve —the <i>resguardo</i>— was born in colonial law not as a title of private, transferable property, but as a communal, corporate, inalienable grant held in collective usufruct. Under the Crown's direct guardianship, this legal institution sought to gather scattered indigenous communities into ordered settlements known as «towns of doctrine», giving them an exclusive and untouchable territory marked out with boundary stones. The aim was threefold: to guarantee the material sustenance of native families with land they could work in rotation; to secure the systematic collection of royal tribute for the Treasury; and to make evangelisation and social control easier for the doctrine priests.

«La conversión e instrucción de los indios en las cosas y misterios de nuestra fe católica y religión cristiana es la causa final con que se dieron los reinos de las Indias a los Reyes Católicos... por consistir en ello el descargo de su Real Conciencia y el cumplimiento de la obligación que se le puso cuando los recibió de la Santa Sede Apostólica».

II Settlement and the gathering at Cananigua (1623)

The reserve policy took definitive shape in the Valley of Labateca during the great general inspection carried out by the judge don Juan de Villabona y Zubiaurre in 1622 and 1623. Villabona, a rigorous follower of Las Casas and a specialist in indigenous law, delegated the work of settlement to the settling magistrate don Luis Jurado —a resident of Pamplona and encomendero of Silos— and to the surveyor don Salvador de Ojeda.

On 19 July 1623 the colonial authorities signed the celebrated «Act of Settlement of the Indians of the Valley of the Madmen». The decree ordered fifteen scattered native groups, fragmented among encomiendas, to be formally gathered into a single settlement: the temperate, healthy plain of Cananigua, today the town centre of Labateca. There the square plaza was laid out with cords, the straight streets six <i>varas</i> wide were drawn, and the encomenderos were ordered to build at their own cost a church of rammed earth on stone foundations, roofed with tile and with a proper sacristy, where the indigenous people would be instructed.

Villabona y Zubiaurre's act did not only organise the town's urban space; it marked out the reserve and community lands with mathematical precision. A total of 9,500 paces of frontage were measured, from which 1,500 paces were set aside as common pasture for livestock and 500 for the town's own plain. The remaining 7,500 paces of workable land were shared out among the groups in proportion to the number of their souls: the Labateca group of Diego de Mora's encomienda, with 107 souls, was given 756 paces of width and frontage, while Gonzalo de Salcedo's group, with 53 souls, received 378 paces.

III The Bourbon crisis and the extinction order of 1778

By the second half of the eighteenth century the demographic picture of the north-eastern New Kingdom had turned around completely. The reserve's indigenous population was falling sharply through intermarriage and the relentless pressure of free settlers. The mass of white and mestizo farmers —the <i>agregados</i>, the attached ones— was meanwhile growing out of all control, renting in fact, or simply seizing, the indigenous people's fertile land.

Against that background, the Crown's inspecting attorney don Francisco Antonio Moreno y Escandón launched in 1778 an aggressive reduction campaign within the Bourbon Reforms. Moreno y Escandón held that keeping vast stretches of land in the hands of a few indigenous people was an obstacle to agricultural progress and a loss to the Royal Treasury. His policy was to extinguish the towns of indians with small populations, move and gather the surviving indigenous people into a few regional centres such as Cácota de Velasco, and declare the lands of the extinguished reserves vacant or crown land, to be auctioned to white farmers.

In August 1778 Moreno y Escandón visited Labateca and, having confirmed that the tributary indigenous population was small against the large neighbourhood of <i>agregados</i>, ordered the Labateca reserve extinguished and its people moved by force. But the indigenous people of Labateca, led by their chiefs and captains, unleashed a formidable legal battle that astonished the Royal Audiencia in Santafé.

They appealed the Bourbon decree, refusing to leave the lands of their ancestors and, above all, their beloved Virgin of Sorrows. The heart of their legal defence was to demonstrate the sheer solidity and economic power of their Marian brotherhood, which in practice worked as a genuine communal agricultural bank. They showed that the brotherhood owned a prosperous herd of more than 187 head of cattle under its own brand on the Virgin's land, a communal cacao plot in excellent production, and more than 5,050 pesos placed at interest —as mortgage credit at 5% a year— on the estates of the wealthiest local landowners.

«Los indios de Labateca demostraron tal solvencia material y económica gracias a las rentas y créditos de su Cofradía de las Angustias, que la Corona española se vio obligada a derogar el auto de extinción de 1778, manteniendo de forma excepcional el resguardo y la doctrina indígena, siendo uno de los últimos reductos de propiedad comunal andina que sobrevivió intacto hasta el siglo XIX».

IV The breaking up of the territory and the separation of Toledo

Although the Labateca reserve survived the Bourbon extinction of 1778, living side by side in one town centre —the mestizo farming population and the indigenous community— became unsustainable by the end of the eighteenth century. The growth of the white population and the narrowness of the town centre produced constant friction, lawsuits over water and damage done by horses to the natives' planted fields.

In February 1790 the white <i>agregados</i>, led by don Juan Manuel de Mora and don Josef Manuel de Cáceres Enciso, formally opened the case for parish separation. Leaning on the Laws of the Indies, they argued the need to found a new settlement at the site of Belén (Toledo), and gave a curious physical reason for it: they claimed that the town centre of Labateca stood on the edge of an unstable ravine, and that with every hard winter the bluff slid and threatened imminent ruin.

On 22 February 1790 the priest don Rafael Jácome y Guerrero, sent specially by the Curia of Santafé, measured the distance and returned the exact figure of 3,750 <i>varas</i> between the plaza of Labateca and the proposed site of El Salado —formerly a pasture of the late Tomás de Mora's holding. After doña Juana Gertrudis de Almeida donated the land and viceroy José de Ezpeleta issued the final decree, Toledo was born as a parish for white people on 14 February 1796, with the course of the river Culagá set as the natural and irreversible boundary between the two jurisdictions. With that, the old Valley of the Madmen was definitively divided.

The passage from reserve to modern municipality took place in the nineteenth century. With the Constitution of Cúcuta in 1821 and the territorial law of 1824, the old categories of indigenous doctrines were abolished and Labateca was absorbed under the legal figure of Parish District. The Constitution of 1886 settled its final status as Municipal District — by which time the reserve, already extinguished in fact by intermarriage and by the privatisation of communal land, had ceased to exist as a legal body and had become the municipality of today, keeping in its depths for ever the courage, the faith and the mysticism of its Chitarero inheritance.
