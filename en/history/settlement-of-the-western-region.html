The settlement of the Western Region

Cities, towns of indians, parishes and modern towns in the Zulia river basin

Silvano Pabón Villamizar, historian (UIS), 2014 · translated with the author's permission

How every settlement in the Zulia basin came to be, from the Spanish cities of the sixteenth century to the coffee towns and the border municipalities of the twentieth — including a correction that removes a founding legend from the textbooks.

Study by Silvano Pabón Villamizar, historian at the Universidad Industrial de Santander, written for the Governor's Office of Norte de Santander (2014) and published here translated with his permission. The original is on the Spanish page.

THE SETTLEMENT OF THE WESTERN REGION

Cities, towns of indians, parishes and modern towns in the basin of the river Zulia. Research and text by Silvano Pabón Villamizar, historian (UIS). Governor's Office of Norte de Santander, Departmental Secretariat of Culture. San José de Cúcuta, Colombia, 2014.

1 Introduction

This text reconstructs in detail the patterns of settlement, the dynamics of occupation and the historical course of the human settlements and territorial units of the Zulia river basin, from the early hispanisation of the sixteenth century to the consolidation of republican life and the modern towns of the nineteenth and twentieth.

Throughout, it analyses systematically how land and natural resources were appropriated, identifying precisely the social and political actors who drove those transformations: the original indigenous communities, the Spanish conquerors and encomenderos, and the farming communities of white and mestizo people. Running through all of it is the fundamental role of the Catholic Church and of communal religiosity as the main engines of urbanisation, territorial ordering and the emergence of the parishes and municipalities that make up the geography of the Western Region today.

2 The Spanish cities and their jurisdiction

The Zulia basin was brought into the orbit of Spanish imperial rule through the establishment of what were called «founded cities». These were the only entities that formally received the legal category of <i>foundation</i>, and they formed the base of the Crown's judicial and administrative structure through their councils, magistracies and regiments.

2.2 · The city of Pamplona de Indias

Founded on 2 November 1549 by the expeditionary companies of the Spanish captains Ortún Velasco de Velázquez and Pedro de Ursúa, Pamplona was raised as the great metropolis and political centre of the north-east of the New Kingdom of Granada. The conquerors, turned into citizens and council members, pacified and distributed in encomienda some hundred indigenous communities scattered across a vast territory reaching from the Chicamocha depression to the foothills of the Mérida range in present-day Venezuela.

Pamplona's early economy rested on the mining boom that followed the discovery of rich gold and silver deposits in the surrounding páramos —Páramo Rico, Vetas and La Montuosa— and, alongside it, on a prosperous farming and cattle enterprise on the temperate slopes and in the cold valleys. On that economic strength the council of Pamplona backed and planned risky military and settlement expeditions, which gave rise to important cities in the region: Mérida (1558), San Cristóbal (1561), Ocaña (1570), La Grita (1576) and Salazar de las Palmas (1583) — confirming Pamplona as a «mother and founder of cities».

2.3 · The city of Salazar de las Palmas

Founded in 1583 by captain Alonso Esteban Rangel, a citizen of Pamplona, Salazar de las Palmas was born with the military and strategic purpose of guarding the royal mule road towards the river ports and of subduing the Motilón and Quiriquire peoples who harassed navigation on the Zulia. The council of Pamplona ceded part of its lands to form the new jurisdiction.

<b>Here Silvano Pabón Villamizar makes a correction of real historiographical weight.</b> Schoolbook tradition and official local accounts have wrongly held that the founding was preceded by the discovery of legendary «Mines of San Pedro» and by a supposed revolt of black and indigenous people led by a captain Diego de Montes. Archival research shows that this account comes from a misreading of the colonial chronicles of friar Pedro de Aguado and friar Pedro Simón: the real «Mines of San Pedro», and the town founded by Diego de Montes in 1554, were on the river Buría in what is today the state of Yaracuy, Venezuela, near Barquisimeto and El Tocuyo — and never had anything to do with the territory of Salazar.

2.4 · The city of San Faustino de los Ríos

Established late, in 1662, by captain Francisco Jimeno de los Ríos, the city and governorship of San Faustino de los Ríos was a frontier bulwark and a forward river port, meant to secure navigation down the Zulia to Lake Maracaibo. Its position made it the obligatory crossing point for the export trade in cacao and flour from Pamplona, Salazar and San Cristóbal.

But because of its unhealthy climate, decimated by fevers, and the constant harassment of the rebel Motilón nations, the town emptied gradually through the eighteenth century and was reduced to bare subsistence. Its leading families —the Colmenares, Omaña, Fortoul and Villafrade— moved their homes for good to the prosperous parishes of San José de Cúcuta and El Rosario, keeping in San Faustino only the far end of their cacao enterprises.

3 The indigenous communities

When the Spanish arrived in the sixteenth century, the wide valley of the Zulia and its tributaries held a rich mosaic of indigenous communities from several peoples. The conquerors divided them by river basin, distinguishing clearly different patterns of dwelling and resources.

<b>1 · The upper reaches</b> (the río de La Plata and Mutiscua). Home to the cold-country Chitarero peoples —Caldera, Tapaguá and Rúbicha— peaceable and easy to live alongside. They were quickly granted in encomienda and drawn in as <i>mita</i> labour for the Andean mining districts and the wheat fields.

<b>2 · The middle reaches</b> (the rivers Cucutilla, Arboledas and Peralonso). The seat of substantial temperate-country Chitarero groups such as Arcabuzazo, Arboledas, Siravita, Zulasquilla and Taqueroma. These communities lived dispersed, building their wattle-and-thatch huts on flagstone foundations along the ravines, and working several altitude zones at once.

<b>3 · The lower reaches and the frontier</b> (the Catatumbo forests and the foothills). Inhabited by Carib and Motilón groups to the north, and by U'wa-Tuneba or Lache communities to the east, along the banks of the Valegrá. These groups kept up a long and warlike resistance to the conquerors' pressure.

<i>The legacy of these communities survives today in abundant archaeological sites in Mutiscua and its hamlets —La Plata, El Chorrerón and Balegrá— where the excavations of the French Eudist Henri Rochereau (1920) and the systematic research of Jairo Calle Orozco (1961) recovered mummies laid out in foetal position, wrapped in woven blankets and fique hammocks, confirming patterns of collective burial in rock crypts high in the mountains.</i>

4 Encomienda and doctrine

The formal subjection of the native population was carried out under the encomienda system, granted as reward to the soldiers of the company that founded Pamplona. The original dispersal of the indigenous people made it hard to collect tribute and to impose evangelisation, which forced the Crown to apply the policy of gathering the natives into «towns of indians» in the Spanish manner.

4.1 · Early settlements at Mutiscua

In the valley of Tapaguá —today Mutiscua— the groups of Caldera, Tapaguá and Rúbicha were put at the service of the encomenderos of Pamplona. Captain Alonso Rangel, encomendero of Tapaguá, established extensive cattle holdings and wheat fields there, building around 1590 a sophisticated watermill for grinding flour. In 1623 the settling magistrate Luis Jurado moved and united these people in the doctrine and reserve of Silos.

5 The parishes of the Zulia basin

Through the eighteenth century, the collapse of the indigenous population coincided with a steep rise in the number of white, mestizo and mulatto people who settled as <i>agregados</i> around the doctrine lands. Far outnumbering and out-resourcing the dwindling indigenous communities, the <i>agregados</i> began the «New Granadan parish movement», petitioning the Royal Audiencia and the archbishopric for the separation and erection of diocesan parishes.

5.1 · The parishes of Arboledas and Cucutilla

By 1772 the Franciscan doctrine of Arboledas had only 12 able men and 52 others —64 souls in all— against a large congregation of 165 attached families totalling 872 souls. The inspector Francisco Antonio Moreno y Escandón decreed the formal extinction and demolition of the town of indians in 1778, moving the few surviving natives to Cúcuta and Chinácota and opening the way for a parish of white people.

The inhabitants of the upper valley of Cucutilla pressed their own case for separation. On 13 May 1802 the parish roll of the vice-parish of Cucutilla was drawn up in the chapel built by father Diego de Buitrago on his old premises, and it showed an overwhelming demographic advantage over Arboledas: 149 married heads of household, 56 single men and widowers, and 37 widows and single women, against only 45 married households in Arboledas. The definitive erection of the parish of the Immaculate Conception of Cucutilla was decreed by the archbishopric of Santafé in 1804 and ratified by viceroy Antonio Amar y Borbón.

5.3 · El Zulia and lower Santiago

A similar process of dismantling took place in the rich cacao lowlands of the Peralonso and the Zulia. The estate owners of the demolished town of Santiago —don Miguel Ramírez Camacho, don Cristóbal Ramírez Camacho, Manuel Orozco and Alonso de Molina— resisted stubbornly the plan of the priest of Salazar, Joseph Ambrosio de Jáuregui, who wanted to concentrate the parish on his own estate at San Cayetano. After long litigation, the Royal Audiencia and the archbishopric authorised a Solomonic solution on 6 March 1778: the parish of San Cayetano del Zulia was erected and, at the same time, the congregation of the Apostle James in the lower reaches was granted the title of vice-parish and a boundary of its own — with the rivers Valegrá and Culagá confirmed as the natural limits of the cacao estates.

6 The modern towns, and the coffee towns

With the end of the wars of independence, the nineteenth century brought a deep economic reconfiguration. After the ruin of the rich traditional cacao groves of the plain under the unstoppable <i>mancha</i> blight, the landed elite of Pamplona and Cúcuta turned their capital to coffee. Coffee growing, markedly commercial and aimed at export through Maracaibo, drove the widening of the agricultural frontier, the opening of mule roads, the laying of the Cúcuta Railway and the systematic urbanisation of the Andean mountains.

6.1 · Mutiscua

The farming population of the Tapaguá valley remained under the civil control of Pamplona and the parish of El Carmen until, in 1841, the inhabitants of Tapaguá, Cupagá, Manzano, Aradita and Los Rastrojos formally asked to be separated. By decree of the Governor's Office of Pamplona of 24 November 1841 the new parish district was erected, with orders to lay out the town on the plain of Hatoviejo, on the provincial road to Santafé — and so it became the prosperous municipality of Mutiscua.

6.2 · Durania, first called San José de Córdoba

The temperate lands once inhabited by the now extinct Operoma and Oquerena peoples were taken over by coffee estates at the end of the nineteenth century, among them Los Alpes and El Resguardo. Around 1898 the smallholders of the hamlets of La Troja and La Lejía formed an improvement committee and built a chapel dedicated to Saint Joseph.

Through the influence of the liberal leader general Justo Leonidas Durán, owner of the El Recreo estate, the departmental assembly issued ordinance no. 12 of 23 March 1911, separating from Bochalema and raising to municipalities the districts of Mundonuevo and Córdoba, under the names Herrán and Córdoba. In 1930, by ordinance 24, the municipality of Córdoba changed its name for good to Durania in honour of general Durán, and in 1935 the building of its masonry parish church began.

6.3 · Gramalote, first San Rafael de Galindo

The spread of Salazar's coffee frontier into the Calderera sector gave rise to a lively hamlet at San Rafael. In October 1864 the Sovereign State of Santander passed the law creating the district of Galindo, named after the liberal soldier Toribio Galindo. The town plan and the building of the parish church of San Rafael de Galindo were led by Gregorio Montes, with the substantial participation of a notable colony of French and Italian immigrants such as José Berti and E. Antonio Delfino.

After the institutional collapse brought by the civil wars and the earthquake, the district changed its name to Gramalote in 1888, moving the town centre to a new layout of plaza and straight streets, with its handsome classical parish church.

6.4 · El Zulia, from «Las Tapias» to a village

Set on the lowlands of the Zulia, on the obligatory stretch of the royal mule road towards Salazar and Ocaña, the hamlet of Las Tapias gained great commercial value as an inland port and staging post for coffee and cacao. In 1850 don Miguel Betancourt's Las Tapias estate was operating as a collection centre for cattle and grain.

From 1888 the notaries of Cúcuta began formally to call the settlement «the village of El Zulia». By ordinance no. 4 of 30 October 1959, issued by the assembly of Norte de Santander, the municipality of El Zulia was definitively erected, bringing together the districts of El Zulia and Pan de Azúcar, separated from Cúcuta.

6.5 · Puerto Santander, the border link

Born under the shelter of the Cúcuta Railway station (northern line) at Bocas del Grita, the settlement took shape after the building in 1926 of the Unión international bridge, which linked the Colombian railway with the Great Táchira Railway in Venezuela. Puerto Santander became the customs and police point with the heaviest commercial traffic in the Catatumbo.

Originally part of the district of Puerto Villamizar, the council of Cúcuta raised it to an independent district in 1947, and finally the departmental assembly issued ordinance no. 80 of 20 December 1993, creating the municipality of Puerto Santander from 1 April 1994 and confirming its strategic place on the Colombian-Venezuelan border.
