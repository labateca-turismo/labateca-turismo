The Chitareros

Pre-Hispanic inhabitants of the old province of Pamplona, in the Sierras Nevadas

Silvano Pabón Villamizar, historian (UIS), 1992 · translated with the author's permission

Who lived here before 1550: their territory, their language, their houses of stone and wattle, their shell-bead necklaces instead of gold — and the sworn testimony of a soldier who described what Pedro de Ursúa's company did in the province of Ima.

Study by Silvano Pabón Villamizar, historian at the Universidad Industrial de Santander, published here and translated with his permission. The 1551 testimony is a colonial court document in the public domain and is translated in full. The original is on the Spanish page.

THE CHITAREROS: PRE-HISPANIC INHABITANTS OF THE OLD PROVINCE OF PAMPLONA, IN THE SIERRAS NEVADAS

A historical, archaeological, demographic and ethnographic study of the inhabitants of Colombia's eastern range.

Research and text by Silvano Pabón Villamizar, historian of the Universidad Industrial de Santander. School of History, UIS, Bucaramanga, Colombia, 1992.

Introduction

Any archaeological and ethnohistorical view of the territory of Santander has to turn to the almost unknown world of the Chitarero people. For all their influence and importance in the pre-Hispanic and Hispanic history of Colombia, they have been pushed into the background of the systematic scientific research they deserve. Even less recognised is the mark the province of Pamplona —the main base of Chitarero settlement— has left on the national historical record.

Archaeological information is still limited, and although the region holds abundant ethnohistorical material, it needs reviewing. What follows are some considerations drawn from an analysis of those sources, offered to open the way to new interdisciplinary work on the Chitarero question and to a fuller study of this region.

The first research in the Pamplona area was begun early in the twentieth century by the French Eudist father Henri Rochereau. He gave priority to describing the Chitareros ethnohistorically, working from the chronicles of the conquest and from his own archaeological fieldwork. His tireless labour led to the organisation of what would become the Archaeological and Historical Museum of Pamplona, many of whose objects are kept today in the city's Casa de la Cultura. Rochereau combined teaching at the Major Seminary with social and anthropological research that lasted more than forty years.

For his part don Luis Eduardo Páez Courvel, the distinguished historian of Norte de Santander, drew on the chroniclers of the conquest and on abundant colonial documentation to provide valuable data and lines of analysis for the Spanish occupation in the first decades of Hispanic life in the province of Pamplona. Don Belisario Mathos Hurtado described the district's mythology, customs and daily life, publishing important colonial documents and weaving epic accounts of conquerors and indigenous people.

To these efforts must be added researchers of the standing of Luis Duque Gómez, who gave a general picture of Chitarero ethnography —economy, housing, magic and religion— and the scholar Próspero María Jaimes with his historical monograph on Guaca. It is in the rigorous work of the historian Germán Colmenares, however, that the greatest quantity of documented information on this people is systematised, with a masterly analysis of how the native population was subjected to the encomienda system and to the land inspections of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

I The Chitarero territory

The groups called Chitareros were located, ethnohistorically, across a significant part of the two Santanders. From the upper basins of the rivers Guaca and Servitá they covered a wide band reaching to the river Suratá, taking in the lands of the colonial mining complex of Vetas and the areas known as Montuosa Alta and Montuosa Baja. The towns of indians in what is today San Andrés, Guaca, Tona, Charta, California, Matanza and Suratá stand out here, as does San José de Miranda —the old Tequia and Carcasí— points identified as possible ethnic frontiers of the region.

From the descriptions of the chronicler Lucas Fernández de Piedrahita we may infer that the Chitarero territory covers what was colonially the old province of Pamplona: from the borders of Vélez, on the river del Oro, forming a wide straight band that passed through San Cristóbal as far as the domains of Mérida in present-day Venezuela. Friar Pedro de Aguado corroborates this in saying that this province is all mountain country, some of it very high — the stretch of the Eastern Range from the Chicamocha depression to where it forks into the Mérida range.

Although the exact frontiers remain a matter for archaeological and ethnolinguistic study, a general geographical frame of pre-Hispanic settlement can be proposed. In Santander it broadly covers the municipalities of Cachirí, Suratá, California, Matanza, Vetas, Charta, Tona, Guaca and Servitá, and the upper parts of Santa Bárbara, Cepitá and Umpalá; in Norte de Santander, Silos, Chitagá, Mutiscua, Cácota, Pamplona, Pamplonita (the old Chopo), Durania, Bochalema, Chinácota, Cucutilla, Toledo, <b>Labateca</b>, Herrán, Ragonvalia, Arboledas, Zulia, Salazar de las Palmas, San Cayetano and Cúcuta — reaching as far as San Cristóbal and the state of Táchira in Venezuela.

II Their neighbours

Because of constant contact and exchange, the precise limits of Chitarero territory border on several peoples of great archaeological importance. To the south lay the frontier with the Guanes, whose point of contact and exchange was set on the lower river Suratá as far as the páramo of Santa Bárbara, at the head of the river Umpalá. That frontier coincides with towns of indians granted in encomienda as Chitareros —Tequia, Mogotocoro and Listará in San Andrés, and the settlements of Tona and Servitá— which casts doubt on the traditional thesis that placed the Guane-Chitarero limit rigidly on the river Guaca.

There was direct and syncretic contact on the bank of the Chicamocha. There the people of the doctrine of Chicamocha, of Muisca descent, shared with the Tequia people, who were Chitarero, an ingenious <i>tarabita</i> or rope crossing — cords stretched from bank to bank with a travelling basket, used to cross the powerful river and exchange goods. The inspector Luis Enríquez recorded in 1602 that both peoples used and enjoyed that crossing in common, which shows that the Chicamocha worked as a dynamic channel of communication rather than an impassable barrier.

On the eastern frontier the Chitareros bordered on Lache and Tunebo (U'wa) groups, settled in the lower, warmer foothills of the Eastern Range and extending towards the rivers Valegrá, lower Chitagá and Ulagá. In 1602 the inspector Luis Enríquez reported at Carcasí a three-ethnicity group in which Chitarero, Lache and Tunebo people lived together. To the north and north-west, Chitarero territory bordered on Carib-speaking groups such as the Motilones —in the northern ranges and the valley of San Faustino— and the Hacaritama, around Ábrego and Ocaña, forming a broad corridor of cultural and commercial exchange.

III Population, language and settlement pattern

3.1 · Population

The demography of these pre-Hispanic communities is hard to establish, because of the immense loss of life in the first years of the violent shock of the conquest, frequently left out of the official Spanish sources. The rolls of the early land inspections do, however, give approximate figures of undeniable value. At the close of the systematic inspection by the notary Cristóbal Bueno in June 1559, a total population of 31,855 indigenous people held in encomienda was recorded in the jurisdiction of Pamplona. The historian Jaime Jaramillo Uribe calculates that the region's original population was more than 60,000 souls when the conquering companies arrived, with some 40,000 concentrated on the rich slopes of Matanza and Pamplona.

3.2 · Language

Contemporary linguistic study classifies Chitarero as a subgroup or variant of the Chibcha family. Though they shared roots with the Muiscas, the Chitareros showed marked dialectal and idiomatic differences. The chronicler friar Pedro de Aguado underlines this in calling the inhabitants of the Valle de los Cercados, in Tequia, «people different in language and in dress». Even so the linguistic affinity with the Muiscas of the highlands was very strong, as Aguado's chronicles show: «their songs, their drinking feasts and their burials are like those of the Muisca indians».

That closeness of language made the Spanish occupation far easier. When general Pedro de Ursúa and his men entered Chinácota and the Valley of the Madmen, they were able to communicate directly with the local chiefs using as interpreters —«tongues»— Muisca servants brought from Tunja. Colonial inspectors used the same method throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, travelling with Muisca translators to speed up the enrolment and assessment of tribute.

3.3 · Settlement and housing

When the Spanish troops arrived, the Chitarero communities had no consolidated urban centres and no grid uniformity. Their settlements were spread widely across the slopes and temperate valleys, following the courses of rivers, streams and brooks in search of plentiful water, forming a kind of dispersed rural neighbourhood. The chronicler friar Pedro de Aguado observed that «most of them live in valleys that incline more to the warm than to the cold», which shows an excellent grasp of several altitude zones at once in the broken geography of the eastern Andes.

Friar Pedro Simón gives the most detailed description of how Chitarero houses were built: «The dwelling consisted of huts, rectangular and square, roofed with thatch, for they did not know the art of the tile; the walls were formed of thick timbers, canework within and without, and set with a mixture they made of mud and straw. The lower half of the walls, from the floor up, they inlaid with stone.» That technique is the direct origin of the region's traditional wattle-and-daub on stone foundations. There was also a defensive variant at Tequia, described by Piedrahita, where the houses of the leading men were ringed with stakes and thickly woven reed canes.

IV Resources and economy

The base of the Chitarero economy and diet was maize, varied according to the altitude of their valleys. In the cold country they successfully grew potatoes, broad beans and cabbages; on the temperate and warm slopes, cassava, arracacha, sweet potato, beans, tomatoes, squash and chilli. They took plentiful wild fruit —avocados, guavas, pineapples, star apples, palm hearts— and honey. Animal protein came from the domesticated <i>curí</i>, a medium-sized rodent, and from constant hunting of deer, rabbits, curassows and guans in the dense Andean forests.

Cotton weaving was highly developed among the Chitareros of Icota, Guaca, Cáraba, Ima, Bochagá and <b>Labateca</b>, who were regarded as excellent manufacturers. They obtained cotton in the temperate lowlands of the region and through a lively trade with the Guanes near the Chicamocha. They wove shirts in the form of tunics reaching to the knee, and square blankets finely decorated with <i>bija</i> or annatto, a red vegetable dye with which they painted both their clothes and their bodies. Peoples in the coldest places, or with less access to the trading networks, replaced cotton with fique or agave fibre for their clothing and gear.

Unlike other peoples of the highlands, the pre-Hispanic Chitareros neither worked nor hoarded gold. To the Spanish they seemed extremely poor in precious metals, for gold played no part in their rites or their personal ornament. Father Rochereau and the chronicles of the conquest record that the chief object of value and prestige among the Chitareros was the necklace of beads made from sea-snail shells. Those shells, acquired by trade with the peoples of the lower Zulia and the Maracaibo basin, were cut into small uniform discs. When Pedro de Ursúa demanded gold from the people of Chinácota, they offered him strings of shells, insisting that «this was their gold» — which Ursúa ordered thrown away in contempt.

V Society and politics

The political and social structure of the Chitareros was marked by the absence of a supreme central chief or a confederation of chiefdoms of the Muisca kind. Friar Pedro de Aguado describes the model clearly: «The natives of this valley had no cacique, nor does any of the province of the indians the Spanish called chitareros have one. The order of government they keep among themselves is that in each town they obey the richest and bravest man, and him they hold as captain in their wars.» Despite the dispersal of their houses, they had a complex network of local authorities —leading men, or captains— who coordinated the sowing, the exchange of blankets and the common defence of the territory.

In war the Chitareros proved highly compact and organised fighters. The conquest reports relate that they fought drawn up in well-ordered squadrons, led by captains who urged them on with great battle cries. On the hills and slopes of their mountain valleys they resisted the Spanish incursion tenaciously, facing down with disciplined courage the harrying of cavalry and war dogs. With the settlement decreed by the judge Juan de Villabona y Zubiaurre in 1623, the Crown restored and reorganised these ethnic hierarchies, gathering the native population into reserve towns laid out in quarters according to their original ties of kinship and dialect.

VI Chitarero archaeology

6.1 · Rochereau's excavations

Father Henri Rochereau's archaeological work in the early twentieth century produced the first material evidence of the Chitarero people. At the Loma de la Cruz, near Pamplona, he excavated a pre-Hispanic cemetery where he identified parallel graves following the natural line of the hill. In them he found abundant necklaces of snail shell, ovoid narrow-necked ceramics, and vessels holding lime — like that which the Muiscas and Tunebos mixed with coca leaf, or <i>ayo</i>, for daily use, evidence of an old channel of exchange across the ranges.

Rochereau proposed three categories of Chitarero burial: first, shaft tombs with one or several side chambers cut into the hills (Loma de la Cruz, Mutiscua, Tona); second, crypts or natural caves in the rocky ledges of the range, used as ossuaries or collective mummy burials (Silos, Mutiscua), where he found four mummies seated in a circle, wrapped in fine cotton blankets tied with cords; and third, deep shaft burials in the flat parts of the valley (Cariongo, in Pamplona), closed at the top with large stone slabs.

6.2 · The archaeology of Mutiscua

The systematic study «Arqueología de Mutiscua», carried out in 1961 by Jairo Calle Orozco and Luis Raúl Rodríguez Lamus in the hamlets of La Plata, Tapaguá, Sicavala and El Chorrerón, is the most scientifically rigorous stratigraphic work on this people. The authors classified the ceramic material recovered into four technological groups —Mutiscua rough red, Mutiscua rough black, Mutiscua incised red and Mutiscua smooth incised black— analysing the composition of the clay, the firing techniques, the diameters and the decoration.

Excavations in the burial caves of Sicavala revealed mummies arranged in a chain, in foetal position, wrapped in fique hammocks and fine blankets painted with ochre dyes and geometric figures. Stone axes and skilfully carved stone idols were also recovered. Within this research the anthropologist Luis Duque Gómez radiocarbon-dated charcoal samples associated with the Mutiscua sites, returning an age earlier than the Christian era — evidence of very old and complex human settlement on these eastern Andean slopes.

Appendix 1 · Findings on the skeletal material

The anatomical and anthropometric study of remains recovered from the Chitarero cemeteries of Pamplona, Silos and Mutiscua, compiled by Calle Orozco and Rodríguez Lamus, returned these constant physiological characteristics:

  • Intentional cranial deformation, common among the peoples of the ranges.
  • Consistently well-developed canine fossae.
  • Notable frequency of a botrocraspedote lower border of the piriform aperture.
  • Presence of a wormian bone in several of the skulls analysed.
  • Persistence of the metopic suture in a significant proportion of individuals.
  • Markedly prominent brow ridges and orbital arches.
  • Presence of the vermian fossa in the occipital region.
  • Strong build and excellent preservation of the teeth and jaw.
  • Recovery of musical instruments —flutes— carved from the bones of wild animals.

Appendix 2 · Findings on the ceramics

Morphological and industrial analysis of the Chitarero ceramic material allows these general typological conclusions about its manufacture and use:

  • Two kinds of ware coexist: a utilitarian, domestic ware of coarse clay and large size, and a ceremonial or ritual ware of better technique, finer paste and thin walls.
  • Incised decoration on the necks of vessels and bowls: horizontal lines, aligned dots and crossed lines forming rhomboid figures.
  • General absence of polychrome painting; the natural finish of the clay predominates —rough red, rough black— with simple liming or smoothing.
  • The typology is confirmed by Calle Orozco's excavations at Mutiscua and by analysis of Rochereau's archaeological collection, held in the Archdiocesan Museum of Pamplona.

Documentary appendix · The 1551 case against Pedro de Ursúa

The secret inquiry conducted by the judge licentiate Alonso de Zorita between 1550 and 1551, held in the General Archive of the Indies (Justicia 561, ff. 174–187), is the most valuable contemporary testimony on the Spanish military incursion into Chitarero territory and the Valley of the Madmen. What follows is the literal transcription of the statement of Alonso Rodríguez de Escobar, soldier and magistrate of the first council of Pamplona, taken under oath in the town of Mompox on 26 June 1551 before the ordinary magistrates Alonso de Carvajal and Gaspar de Heredia.

<b>Asked whether he was present at the campaign and pacification of the provinces of the Sierras Nevadas and Pamplona, he said:</b> That this witness went with the said Pedro de Ursúa to the said provinces of the Sierras Nevadas and Pamplona, to the conquest and pacification of the said province and its indians… And that they entered the land in the year forty-nine, in the month of October, and on the first day of it, All Saints' Day, the town called the city of Pamplona was founded in the Valley of All Saints, having found food in abundance and some indians then coming to serve.

<b>Asked about the killings of natives committed by the Spanish in the province of Ima, he declared:</b> That this witness went out with the General to range over the land and inspect it, and saw that in the province of Ima, one day at about ten in the morning, the said General arrived with sixty or seventy men; and the indians were going about the hills, and it was not known whether they were at peace or at war, since they had all their women and children and goods in their houses and most of them carried no arms but clubs; and the Spanish fell upon them in a skirmish in which it seems to this witness that there died that day, by lance and sword and dog, thirty or forty souls, more or less; besides the children whom the servant indians threw from the cliffs, taking them from their mothers' arms, and taking the women for their own use; and these indians who did this were indians long trained in Spanish service, and they killed the said children so as to have better service from the mothers — for this witness saw some of those children crying, and saw them afterwards dead on the rocks, thrown down.

<b>Asked about the indians' resistance to Ursúa's demands, he declared:</b> He saw that the said General answered them through the said interpreter —one Antonillo, an indian trained in Spanish— that there was no place for them to go from there, but that he would loose the dogs on them, and that they were to tell the chief to come in peace and bring much gold, and that if they did not leave that place the dogs and the horses would grow very angry and would eat them; and the said indians cared nothing for any of this and stayed where they were, because for fifteen days they had neither eaten nor drunk anything but tobacco, and from this they were all drunk and out of their senses…

<b>Asked about the siege of the caves and the use of the attack dogs, he related:</b> Going down along some slopes he came upon indians in caves, with their children and wives and goods, and from the caves they shot an arrow at a black servant of the General's… and then the General ordered men on foot and on horseback to be made ready with dogs… and at the first cave they broke the indians… and from these towns they took away many indians for service, and crossed a páramo of two leagues…
